## Which tier is the host actually selling you?

Four names get used interchangeably in marketing copy, and they are not the same product.

| Tier | Network | What it behaves like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChinaNet/163 | AS4134 | Cheap, huge capacity, the default for most clouds. Congests every evening when residential traffic peaks. |
| CN2 GT | AS4809 Global Transit | Built to escape that congestion, managed it for a while. Since roughly 2019 it is close to 163 in peak-hour performance while still costing substantially more. |
| CN2 GIA | AS4809 Global Internet Access | China Telecom's most expensive transit, quoted as high as $120 per megabit in some markets. Separate paths, fewer hops, priority handling. |
| CTGNet | AS23764 | Newest option, performs in the same range as GIA at similar pricing. |

The GIA price is the part that explains the plans. A full 1 Gbps commit at $120/Mbps would be a six-figure monthly bill, so no budget host puts you on an unshared gigabit of GIA. They cap the port, cap the traffic, or both. Akile's own price list shows the trade cleanly: the GIA line gives you 500 Mbps and 300 GB for ¥34.99, while the international Lite line gives you 5 Gbps and 1 TB for ¥8.88 at the same location. Same company, same city, four times the price for a tenth of the port speed. That gap is the routing.

CN2 GT is the trap to avoid. Its whole pitch was congestion relief, and the relief stopped arriving years ago. If a host advertises "CN2" without the GIA or CTGNet suffix and charges more than plain 163, you are paying a premium for a route that behaves like the cheap one.

## Three carriers, not one

China has three mobile networks, each with its own premium backbone:

- **Telecom:** AS4809 (CN2 GIA) or AS23764 (CTGNet)
- **Unicom:** AS9929 or AS10099 (China Unicom Premium)
- **Mobile:** CMIN2, AS58807

A host that optimizes only Telecom sends Unicom and Mobile visitors home over congested public peering, which is a common way to sell a "China optimized VPS" that only works for part of China. Akile's Los Angeles GIA listing claims all three: CN2 GIA for Telecom, 9929 for Unicom, CMIN2 for Mobile.

That listing also carries a caveat worth reading twice. Telecom traffic currently travels over the Unicom 9929 path while the CN2 GIA route awaits Telecom whitelisting. In practice you may be buying a two-carrier-optimized route with Telecom riding Unicom's premium line. Ask support whether Telecom is on GIA today before you pay a GIA premium.

Compare that with the wording on a European host's China page, which promises "China Telecom (CN2/Direct), China Unicom (AS4837/AS9929), China Mobile". AS4837 is Unicom's ordinary backbone, not the premium 9929, and the Telecom side names no tier at all. When a page will not name the tier, assume the cheapest one.

## Port speed is the spec people forget to read

Two plans can both say "CN2 GIA" and differ by a factor of fifty on throughput. HostDare's CKVM1 runs a **50 Mbps** port at $55.99/year; its CKVM4 is 100 Mbps at $65.99/month. BandwagonHost's Los Angeles E-Commerce line starts at 2.5 Gbps and reaches 10 Gbps on the top plans. Akile's GIA line is 500 Mbps across the board, and its Lite line goes to 5 Gbps.

A 50 Mbps port is roughly 6 MB/s of sustained transfer. Fine for a text site or a shell, painful for anything that serves images or video to several people at once, and irrelevant if your bottleneck is the China return path anyway. Match the port to the workload: proxies and browsing care about port speed and traffic allowance, applications with a database care about RAM and cores, media delivery cares about traffic volume first.

Traffic caps matter as much as the port. Akile throttles to 10 Mbps once you pass the monthly allowance, which keeps the server online but makes it useless for anything interactive until the reset or the next billing cycle. If you expect to run near the cap, buy the plan with headroom rather than paying for resets.

Every host in this market advertises a money-back guarantee, and most of them attach conditions that void it exactly when you would want to use it.

## How to tell a real coupon from a same-name brand's

Coupon aggregators poison this niche. A documented example: codes floating around as "AKKO15", "NEW2026" and "VPS20" for a host called AkkoCloud belong to Akko, a mechanical keyboard brand. Same letters, unrelated company. The reviewer who checked them could not find a single working AkkoCloud-specific code from an authoritative source.

The test is boring and reliable. A code is real if it applies at checkout on the vendor's own cart, on the plan you actually want, at the billing cycle you actually want. A coupon site listing it is not evidence. A code that only cuts the first invoice is a one-time discount; recurring discounts, which apply on renewal, are the ones that matter for a box you keep for two years.

Akile's pricing moves with promotions, and the annual cycle carries the discount (that ¥8.88 headline is an annual-billing figure). Check what the monthly rate is before deciding the annual price is a bargain.
